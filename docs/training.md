# Getting started with Porchlight

*For a recruiter on day one. Read it alone, at your desk, in about twenty minutes. You need a Porchlight login and an agency somebody has already created — if you have neither, ask whoever runs recruitment.*

*Want the bigger picture first? [overview.md](overview.md). Want the full mechanics? [workflow.md](workflow.md).*

---

## Before you touch anything

One idea holds up this entire tool:

> **A person you met once is worth remembering for years.**

Every other system in child welfare wakes up when somebody submits an application. Porchlight wakes up at the fair. The forty people you talk to on a Saturday are not leads to be closed this quarter — most of them are homes that arrive in 2028, and only if somebody remembers them.

Which leads to the second idea, and it will feel wrong at first:

> **"Not yet" is the most valuable answer you will hear all week.**

Not "yes" — "yes" is rare, and you would have remembered it anyway. *"Ask me when my youngest is in school"* is a person telling you exactly when to come back. In every other tool that answer disappears. Here it becomes a date with a name on it, and the system taps you on the shoulder when it arrives.

If you take one habit from this document: **ask for the date.**

## The words

Six stages. You will use these constantly:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Unaware** | Fostering has not crossed their mind yet. |
| **Curious** | They stopped at your table and gave you a way to reach them. |
| **Considering** | They are genuinely weighing it. *Am I eligible? Can we afford it?* |
| **Not yet** | *"Ask me in two years."* A promise with a date. Not a no. |
| **Inquiry** | They raised their hand. Licensing takes it from here. |
| **Licensed** | A home. |

And three words that trip everybody up in their first week:

**Source** — the *place*, not the person. A church fair. A foster mom sharing her link. A radio ad. Every contact belongs to exactly one source, forever, and it cannot be changed later. This is not bureaucracy: it is the only reason anyone will ever be able to prove your Saturdays were worth it.

**Touch** — any real interaction. A phone call, a text, a conversation at a table, an email that went out. Both the ones you have and the ones the system has.

**Wake-up date** — the day the waiting room taps you on the shoulder about somebody. You pick it. It survives everything.

## Your first fifteen minutes

1. **Sign in.** Go to the app and enter your work email. Porchlight sends you a link; clicking it signs you in. **There is no password** — this is deliberate, not a missing feature. Nothing to forget, nothing to leak, nothing to share.
2. **Look at the dashboard.** Four totals across the top, a getting-started checklist that ticks itself off as you go, and *"What needs you today"* — the tasks waiting on a human.
3. **Open the board.** Five lanes. Notice where *Not yet* sits: in the middle, between Considering and Inquiry, not off to the side. That placement is the whole philosophy.
4. **Open one contact.** Their source, their consent, their whole timeline — every conversation, every stage change, every email the system sent, in one column.
5. **Open the ledger,** even though it will look empty or thin. That is the page your director will eventually take to a board meeting. Knowing what it wants from you explains most of the rest of your job.

## Your first event, start to finish

### Before you go — five minutes, the night before

Create the source at `/events`. Name it something you will recognise in eighteen months (*"St. Anne's fall festival, Mesa"* — not *"fair"*).

Then fill in the two fields everybody wants to skip:

- **What it cost.** The table fee, the printing, the giveaways. If it genuinely cost nothing, put zero — that is a real answer.
- **How many hours.** Yours and anyone else's, including setup and drive time.

Skip these and that event is free forever in your ledger. A free event that produces nothing can never be recognised as a wasted Saturday, and a cheap event that produces four homes never gets the credit that would have earned it a bigger budget next year.

Then open the event and **print the QR code.** Test it on your own phone before you leave the office.

### At the table

Somebody stops. You talk. As it winds down:

> *"Can I keep in touch? It's one field, takes ten seconds."*

Point at the QR code, or hand them your phone with the page already open. They enter a phone number **or** an email — either alone is enough — tick the consent box, and they are yours. Name is optional and comes second, because asking for a name first makes it feel like a form.

**When somebody will not scan anything** — and plenty will not — do not lose them. Open the event page and quick-add them yourself. About eight seconds: number, name, done. A name in Porchlight beats a business card in your bag every single time.

Try to catch the *"maybe someday"* people especially. They are the ones every other agency loses.

### Monday morning

Open the event. Everybody you met is there, stamped with where you met them.

Now the part that takes ten minutes and pays for the whole event: go through them and set stages. Most stay *curious*. The ones who were genuinely weighing it go to *considering*. The ones who said "not now" go to **not yet — with the date they gave you.**

## The daily ten minutes

Open `/tasks`. Every morning. That is the habit.

Four kinds of thing land there:

- **Wake-ups** — somebody's date arrived. Call them.
- **Replies** — a family wrote back. Automation has already stopped for them; a human needs to answer.
- **Cold flags** — somebody in *considering* has heard nothing from anyone for thirty days.
- **Monthly outcome check** — families sitting at *inquiry* for a while. Any of them licensed yet?

Then log the conversations you actually had, in a sentence or two.

Be clear about the division of labour: **the nightly job does the machine part only.** It wakes people up, sends the stage-keyed notes, and flags the ones going quiet. It cannot pick up a phone. A task sitting untouched for a week is a family nobody called — and the software will not do it for you.

## Saying "not yet" well

This is the skill that separates a recruiter using Porchlight from a recruiter using a spreadsheet with extra steps.

**Ask for a date, not a maybe.** When somebody says "not right now," the next question is the whole job:

> *"Totally fair. When do you think might be a better time to check back — next spring? After the baby's in school?"*

People answer that question. They almost never volunteer it.

**Then use the date they actually gave you.** When you move somebody to *Not yet*, Porchlight asks *"Check back on"* and offers a year from today. That default is a safety net so nobody sits in the waiting room without a clock — it is not a recommendation. If they said "after my daughter graduates in May," put May. The tapping-on-the-shoulder is only useful if it happens at the right moment.

**Do not apologise for it, and do not treat it as a no.** They will get one quiet, no-pressure note every ninety days — nothing asked of them, nothing to click. That is the porch light. Some of the best homes your agency ever licenses will come from this lane.

## Logging a conversation

Two lines beat memory. Always.

Open the contact, log the touch. *"Called, left voicemail."* *"Talked 20 min — worried about the spare bedroom rule. Sending her the eligibility note."*

Three reasons it matters more than it feels like it does:

1. **The timeline becomes the whole relationship.** Every conversation, every automated email, every stage change, in order. Two years later somebody — maybe not you — can read it in fifteen seconds and pick up exactly where it left off.
2. **Silence is measured.** The cold-flag task fires on "no contact for thirty days." If you had the conversation but never logged it, the system thinks they have been ignored.
3. **You will not be here forever.** The next recruiter inherits what you wrote down, and nothing else.

Notes are for context. Logged conversations are for the record — and once logged, a conversation cannot be edited or deleted. Write it like it is going in a file, because it is.

## When somebody asks to be removed

Two different requests. Listen for which one you are getting.

**"Stop emailing me."** → Unsubscribe. The stage history and the record of where you met them stay; the emails stop. Every nurture email carries an unsubscribe link, so people often do this themselves and you will simply see it happen.

**"Delete my information."** → Erasure, from the danger zone on their contact page. It removes the person completely — contact, conversations, stage history, checklist, everything queued. It is recorded as having been done, so you can answer for it.

**One warning: opting somebody out cannot be undone.** Not by you, not by your director, not by anybody. If you are unsure which one a person is asking for, ask them before you click.

Erasure keeps the *event* and what it cost. That is intentional — it means the ledger stays honest even after somebody exercises a right they are fully entitled to.

## Ten mistakes almost everybody makes once

| The mistake | What to do instead |
|---|---|
| Creating a contact and picking any source just to get past the field | Create the real source, even retroactively. A wrong source is worse than a slow entry — and it can never be corrected later. |
| Leaving cost and hours blank on an event | Fill them in before you go. Blank means free forever, and free events can never be judged. |
| Treating *not yet* as a polite no | It is the highest-value answer you get. Ask for the date and put them in the lane. |
| Accepting the default one-year wake-up every time | Use the date they gave you. Default only when they truly gave you nothing. |
| Having a great phone call and not logging it | Two lines, right after. Otherwise the system reads that family as ignored. |
| Marking somebody *licensed* because the checklist finished | The checklist is your visibility into where a family is stuck. The state licenses families; a human records it here. |
| Letting the task queue pile up for a week | Ten minutes a day. A wake-up task is a promise you made to a real person on a specific date. |
| Expecting the ledger to prove something in month two | Licensure lags first contact by one to two years. Early on, judge yourself on captures and how many people are being held warm. |
| Asking for a name first at the table | Number or email first. Name second, optional. Every extra field loses people. |
| Assuming somebody else will follow up on a reply | A reply pauses automation on purpose. If no human answers, nobody does. |

## Your first week

- [ ] Signed in and found the board, a contact, and the ledger
- [ ] Read the six stages closely enough to use them without checking
- [ ] Created one real source with its real cost and real hours
- [ ] Tested the QR code on your own phone
- [ ] Captured five real people — at least one by quick-add
- [ ] Moved somebody to *Not yet* with a date they actually gave you
- [ ] Logged three real conversations
- [ ] Cleared the task queue twice
- [ ] Opened the ledger once and found your own event on it

Nine things. If you do all nine, you know the tool.

## Two more things

**One account, one agency.** Yours is tied to your work email. Everyone at your agency sees the same contacts, the same waiting room and the same ledger — "director" and "recruiter" are labels, not different levels of access. Assume a colleague can see anything you write.
